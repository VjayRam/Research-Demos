How TurboQuant squeezes a KV cache down to 2–4 bits per number

Random rotation turns any vector — even an adversarial one — into coordinates with a known, fixed statistical shape. Once the shape is known in advance, you can solve for the provably optimal quantizer once, offline, and reuse it forever. That's the entire idea. Everything below derives from it, worked out in formulas you can step through by hand.

arXiv:2504.19874 · TurboQuant arXiv:2406.03482 · QJL arXiv:2502.02617 · PolarQuant

rotate → snap each coordinate to its nearest codebook centroid → rotate back. Norm is preserved; direction drifts by a controlled, bounded amount.
00 · The problem

A KV cache is just a pile of vectors you must shrink

Every attention layer stores a key and a value vector per token, per layer, per head. At fp16, a single d=128 vector costs 256 bytes. Multiply by tens of thousands of tokens and dozens of layers and the cache dwarfs the model weights. The fix is scalar quantization: represent each coordinate with a few bits instead of sixteen. The hard part is choosing which few bits, for data you haven't seen yet, without a calibration pass.

Data-oblivious, by construction. TurboQuant needs no calibration set, no per-tensor scale/zero-point, no Hessian statistics. It works identically on the first token of a brand-new stream as on the ten-thousandth. That constraint — solved once, applied forever — is what shapes every design choice below.

01 · The core trick

Rotation converts an unknown input into a known distribution

Take any unit vector x ∈ Sd-1 — worst case, even adversarially chosen. Multiply it by a random orthogonal matrix Π (the paper's construction: QR-decompose a random Gaussian matrix). Rotations preserve the uniform measure on the sphere, so Πx is still uniform on Sd-1 — regardless of what x was. And a uniform point on a hypersphere has a coordinate distribution you can write down in closed form:

fX(x)  =  Γ(d/2) / (√π · Γ((d−1)/2)) · (1 − x²)(d−3)/2,   x ∈ [−1, 1]

a scaled Beta density that converges to N(0, 1/d) as d → ∞. In high dimensions the paper further notes coordinates become almost independent — enough to quantize each one with its own scalar quantizer and lose little to ignored correlation.

Notice: no matter how concentrated x starts — even a single 1 and the rest zeros — Πx lands close to the theoretical curve. That curve is the same curve for every input. It's this predictability that lets the next step precompute a quantizer once.

02 · The optimal scalar quantizer

Lloyd–Max: continuous 1-D k-means over a known density

Quantizing one rotated coordinate to b bits means choosing 2b centroids to minimize expected squared error under fX:

C(fX, b) := min ∑i ∫bucket i |x − ci|² · fX(x) dx

Because the density is fixed and known ahead of time, this reduces to classic Lloyd's algorithm: boundaries sit at midpoints between neighboring centroids; each centroid is recomputed as the conditional mean of its bucket; repeat to convergence. It depends only on (d, b) — never on the data — so it's solved once and cached forever, exactly the _codebook_cache pattern any real implementation uses.

The information-theoretic floor (Shannon Lower Bound, via a hypersphere test-channel argument, combined with Yao's minimax principle) is Dmse(Q) ≥ 4−b for any quantizer whatsoever. TurboQuant's bound sits at roughly 2.7× that floor in the worst case — and only 1.45× away at b=1. A fixed, fifteen-line, calibration-free quantizer is within a small constant factor of information-theoretic optimality.

A deliberate omission. Each bucket's occupation probability is known exactly from the same solve, so entropy-coding the indices would shave the effective bit-width down toward the codebook's entropy (~3.8 bits at b=4, roughly 5% smaller). The paper explicitly skips this — simplicity and speed over squeezing out the last few percent.

03 · Putting it together

Algorithm 1 — TurboQuantmse

Pseudocode, verbatim structurefrom the paper
setup (once per (d,b), data-independent):
  1  Π ← QR( random Gaussian d×d matrix )
  2  centroids c1..2^b ← Lloyd–Max solve of Eq.(4)

Quantmse(x):    # x unit-norm
  y ← Π · x
  for j in 1..d:  idxj ← argmink |yj − ck|
  return idx    # d indices, b bits each

DeQuantmse(idx):
  ẏ ← cidx  (componentwise lookup)
  return Π⊤ · ẏ    # orthogonal ⇒ Π⊤ = Π&supminus;¹

A non-unit vector stores ‖x‖₂ as one extra float and rescales after dequantization — one scalar for the whole vector, not a per-block scale/zero-point pair per number.

Two practical substitutions every real system makes, including community implementations: replacing the dense O(d²) QR-rotation with an O(d log d) Fast Walsh–Hadamard Transform plus random sign flips, and replacing brute-force argmin over centroids with binary search over sorted boundaries (O(b) instead of O(2b)). Both are algorithmically equivalent in outcome — faster paths to the same distribution and the same nearest-centroid answer.

04 · Fixing a bias

Algorithm 2 — TurboQuantprod, and why it needs QJL

Algorithm 1 minimizes MSE, not inner-product error. At b=1 the optimal centroids are ±√(2/π)/√d, so dequantization is a fixed shrink-and-sign operation. Plugging that into E[⟨y, DeQuant(Quant(x))⟩] gives exactly:

E[⟨y, x̃⟩]  =  (2/π) · ⟨y, x⟩     ≈ 0.6366 × ⟨y,x⟩

The MSE-optimal quantizer systematically shrinks estimated inner products by about 36% at one bit. For attention scores, that's a bias you can't calibrate away after the fact.

The fix: quantize with one fewer bit, then correct the leftover
Quantprod(x)    # (b−1)-bit MSE stage + 1-bit QJL correction
  idx ← Quantmse(x)   at bit-width (b−1)
  r ← x − DeQuantmse(idx)   # small residual
  qjl ← sign(S · r)   Sij ~ N(0,1)  # 1 bit / coord
  return (idx, qjl, ‖r‖₂)

DeQuantprod(idx, qjl, ρ):
  x̃mse ← DeQuantmse(idx)
  x̃qjl ← √(π/2)/d · ρ · S⊤·qjl
  return x̃mse + x̃qjl

Why does correcting only the residual with random signs restore unbiasedness? Decompose the other vector y relative to x into a parallel part y∥ = (⟨y,x⟩/‖x‖²)x and an orthogonal part y⊥. Project both through the same random direction s:

Why the orthogonal part cancels — the geometry behind QJL's proof

Drag the angle between x and y. s·x and s·y⊥ are independent zero-mean Gaussians whenever y⊥ ⊥ x — so E[sign(s·x) · (s·y⊥)] = 0 for any single random direction s, and only the parallel component survives in expectation.

What remains is a known constant: E|N(0,σ²)| = σ√(2/π), the half-normal mean. The √(π/2) in DeQuantqjl is chosen precisely to cancel it, leaving E[⟨y,x̃⟩] = ⟨y,x⟩ exactly — unbiased, at the cost of variance ≤ (π/2)·‖y‖²/d.

QJL on a residual is a different regime than QJL on a raw vector. The standalone QJL paper applies sign(S·k) directly to a whole key vector, and — because quantizing both sides of an inner product to signs only recovers the angle, and cos(·) is nonlinear — keeps the query un-quantized (S·q stays real-valued). TurboQuant instead applies the same 1-bit sign trick only to whatever's left over after an accurate (b−1)-bit stage, so the residual's norm is already small. That's also, independently, why real systems give keys more bits than values: key errors get exponentiated inside softmax, value errors just average out in a weighted sum.

05 · The companion technique

PolarQuant — the same insight, in polar coordinates

PolarQuant is not a third stage bolted onto TurboQuant — the papers benchmark it as a parallel, alternative pipeline, sharing the same root idea: a random preconditioning step makes an otherwise-arbitrary vector look like a draw from a known, favorable distribution. Where TurboQuant exploits that in Cartesian coordinates, PolarQuant exploits it in a recursive polar decomposition.

Recursive Cartesian → polar decomposition, d = 8

Pair coordinates → one radius + one angle per pair → recurse on the radii. log₂d levels turn a length-d vector into one final radius and d−1 angles.

For a preconditioned (near-Gaussian) vector, the joint density of (r, θ(1),…,θ(log₂d)) factorizes completely. Level-1 angles are uniform on [0,2π). Every deeper level concentrates around π/4:

f(θ) ∝ sin2ℓ−1−1(2θ),   θ ∈ [0, π/2],   level ℓ ≥ 2
Angle concentration by recursion depth

Deeper levels are almost deterministic — near π/4 with vanishing variance — so a couple of bits pin them down precisely. The same Lloyd–Max recipe from §02 reapplies here, just against this sin-power density instead of the Beta density.

The payoff: with O(log(1/ε)) bits per coordinate plus one scalar norm for the whole vector, PolarQuant reaches expected distortion ε·‖x‖² — the same rate an ε-net over the sphere would need, without ever building or searching that combinatorially large net.

The papers never work a small example numerically — every reported number is asymptotic or measured at real scale (d=128, d=1536). This one is small enough to trace by hand, using the paper's own asymptotic 1-bit centroid formula c = ±√(2/π)/√d and a concrete Hadamard rotation as a stand-in orthogonal matrix.

Step through it

Input x = (1,0,0,0) is the worst case for naive per-axis quantization — all mass on one coordinate. After rotation every coordinate is a moderate, equal 0.5: the concentration effect from §01, in miniature.

Final error here (‖x−x̃‖² ≈ 0.041) lands comfortably under the paper's expected-case bound of 0.36 — expected, since that bound averages over random rotations and worst-case inputs, while this is one hand-picked sample under one specific rotation. It isn't evidence the bound is loose.

Watch the all-ones (DC) direction: a vector whose mass leans toward a shared mean concentrates disproportionately onto the first Hadamard coefficient, because the fixed Hadamard pattern isn't actually random. That's precisely why practical systems randomize signs before the Hadamard transform — it restores the no-privileged-axis guarantee that a true QR-random rotation gives for free.

08 · What it buys you

Bit-rate and compression ratio

Fractional bit-widths (2.5, 3.5 — the paper's real experiments) come from splitting channels into an outlier set and a bulk set, running Algorithm 1/2 at two different integer bit-widths, and averaging: e.g. 32 outlier channels at 3 bits + 96 bulk channels at 2 bits over d=128 averages to exactly 2.5 bits. Not a different algorithm — mixed precision built from the same integer-bit quantizer, per channel instead of per layer.
